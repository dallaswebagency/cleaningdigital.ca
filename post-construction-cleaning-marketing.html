Problems we solve

Where post-construction cleaning companies lose enquiries.

Contractors cannot see your scope

A line saying you do construction cleanup tells a site manager nothing. They want to know whether you handle rough, final and touch-up stages, how you deal with glass and fixtures, and what is left for them. A vague page sends them to the company that spells it out.

No proof of the kinds of projects you have done

Builders want to know if you have cleaned a townhouse complex, a retail fit-out or a medical build. Without project descriptions and photos, they assume you have not and move on, even if you have done exactly that.

Insurance and safety details are missing

General contractors often need proof of coverage and safety training before anyone enters a site. If this is not obvious, you create friction at the first step, and a competitor who lists it plainly gets the call.

Work depends on a few contractors

Many companies live off one or two builders. If a relationship cools or a project ends, the pipeline dries up quickly, and there is no steady stream of new contacts coming from search or outreach.

Homeowners and builders get the same message

A renovating homeowner and a developer want different things. A page that speaks to both blurs the message for each, so neither feels you are the right fit for their kind of job. Splitting the message in two lets each reader see themselves quickly.

How post-construction cleaning companies win work online

A homeowner rarely searches for a post-construction cleaner. The general contractor does. A site is nearing handover, drywall dust has settled on every ledge and the client walkthrough is days away, so someone gets on the phone and asks who can come in quickly and do it properly. That someone is usually a project manager or site super with a long list, and the first company that answers confidently and sends clear details tends to get the work. Speed matters here, though looking like a safe pair of hands matters just as much.

That makes this one of the most relationship-driven niches in cleaning. Builders, renovators, developers and property managers hire the same crew again when the first job goes well. Your website is rarely the place a deal is signed, but it is where they check you out before the first call. They want to see the type of projects you have handled, how you stage the work and whether you carry the insurance their site requires. In other words, the website is a reference check, not a sales pitch, and it should be built for that purpose.

There is a second, quieter audience. Homeowners finishing a renovation, a basement or a kitchen refit do search online, often after a contractor says they do not clean to a final standard. They need reassurance that the dust, residue and stickers will be gone and that you will not scratch the new floors. A separate page for them keeps the language plain. Their questions are simpler than a builder's, mostly about timing, what is included and whether you work around furniture and flooring that is already in place. Answering those plainly on a page of their own helps.

Search behaviour is specific. People type phrases tied to the stage of the job, such as rough clean, final clean, or construction cleaning for a named kind of building. Matching those stages with clear pages, and showing the checklist you work through, makes it easier for a busy site manager to see exactly what they would be getting. A short intro paragraph on each stage page, followed by the checklist, is usually enough. Add a note on how many people you send and how long a typical visit runs, so a busy site manager can plan around you without calling to ask.

Opportunities

Where the growth is.

Pages for each stage of the job

Rough clean, final clean and touch-up before handover can each have a page that explains what is included and when to book. This matches how project managers think and search, and makes your quote request more specific.

A project portfolio built for builders

Short write-ups of the building type, the size of the crew and the timeline give contractors something to compare against their own project. A few good photos beat a long list of claims. Keep each write-up to a short paragraph so it can be read between site visits.

Builder and developer outreach

A short, professional introduction, backed by a site that looks established, can reach local builders, renovators and developers directly. Work is often won by being on the shortlist before a project reaches its end. It also works as a quiet reminder to past clients, since you stay in their inbox without a hard sell.

Renovation homeowners

A separate page written for people finishing a renovation can capture those who search after the trades leave. It speaks to dust, paint splatter and protecting new surfaces, in plain language. That gives you a second income stream when new builds slow down.

Property managers and new buildings

New units, tenant fit-outs and vacated spaces all need a clean before someone moves in. A page aimed at managers and landlords opens a steady stream of smaller repeat jobs between big projects. Because these jobs are usually smaller, they also fill gaps between large projects.

Across Canada

Post-construction cleaning follows where the building is happening, and that changes by region. Fast-growing cities and their suburbs have a constant stream of new homes, townhouses and commercial units, which suits a company that wants volume and a standing relationship with builders. Smaller centres see more renovations and the occasional large project, so the work is steadier but needs wider travel. Resort towns and mining or energy regions add large camps and lodges, where the work arrives in bursts and travel is part of the quote.

Climate matters more than people expect. Where winters are long, a lot of finishing work happens indoors, and dust, salt tracked in on boots and drywall residue all need removing before handover. In milder coastal areas, outdoor work and glass cleaning can run most of the year. Mud and slush in spring add a rough clean step that the same company in a dry city would rarely need. Those seasonal differences are worth a line on your site, because they show you have done this before.

Building types vary as well. High-rise condo towers need floor by floor scheduling and elevator booking, while single family subdivisions are about speed and volume. Heritage renovations in older neighbourhoods need gentle products and careful handling of original finishes. Your site should show the building types you handle best, so that builders in those areas recognise your work. Our commercial cleaning page covers the ongoing contract side. Builders in each area will recognise the buildings you describe. That recognition is half of winning the call.

Who actually hires a post-construction cleaner?

Mostly general contractors, builders, renovators and developers, often through a project manager or site supervisor. Property managers and homeowners hire too, but less often. Because the buyer is usually a professional, your website should answer their practical questions first: scope, insurance, timing and the kind of buildings you have worked on.

What should be on my services page?

Break the work into stages, such as rough clean, final clean and touch-up, and list what each covers. Mention glass, fixtures, floors and debris removal if you handle them. A clear checklist helps a site manager picture the job and lets them write a more accurate quote request, which saves you both time.

Do I need to show insurance details on my site?

A short, clear note is enough. Contractors often need proof of coverage and safety training before work begins, so mentioning that you carry it, and that certificates are available on request, removes a step from the process. Never publish anything you cannot back up, and keep the wording factual.

How can I get work from builders without cold calling?

Start by looking established. A tidy site, a project gallery and a profile with real photos give a builder something to check after they hear your name. Then a short, polite introduction by email, referring to a specific type of project they build, goes much further than a generic pitch.

Should I market to homeowners too?

If you want those jobs, give them their own page. A person finishing a renovation has different worries from a project manager, such as scratched floors, leftover dust and paint marks. Plain language and a simple quote form suit them. Keep the builder content separate so each audience hears what matters to them.

Is Google Ads useful for this niche?

Sometimes. Searches for construction cleaning are fewer and more specific than for home cleaning, so campaigns stay small. They can help fill gaps between contracts, especially for final cleans that need booking quickly. We start with a modest budget, track calls and forms, and stop anything that does not lead to real conversations.

How do I show work when clients do not allow photos?

Describe the project instead. Note the building type, the stage, the size of the crew and the approach, without naming the client or location. A few photos of your own equipment, team and finished interiors can fill the gap. Always get permission before publishing anything that identifies a site.

Can the same site cover ongoing cleaning contracts?

Yes, as long as the pages are kept distinct. Construction cleanup and ongoing janitorial work attract different buyers and different questions. We usually build separate sections so each reads clearly, then link them for people who need both. Our contact page is the best place to talk through how that would look for your company.
