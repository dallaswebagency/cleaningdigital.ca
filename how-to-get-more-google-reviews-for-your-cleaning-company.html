How to Get More Google Reviews for Your Cleaning Company

By Nakul Chandra, Founder and Lead Strategist · November 11, 2026 · 12 min read

Book a callEmail us

Ask a cleaning company owner what they wish they had more of and a lot of them say reviews. Your customers are letting a stranger into their home or building, so they read what other people said before they ever pick up the phone. A few good reviews can settle that nervous moment in your favour.

The good news is that reviews are not luck. They come from a routine: ask at the right moment, make it easy, reply to everyone and handle the occasional bad one calmly. None of it needs a big budget or any pushy tactics.

This guide gives you the whole routine, including sample texts you can copy, reply templates for good and bad reviews, a step list for a difficult review and a checklist you can hand to your office person. It sits alongside the work we do on your Google Business Profile, where reviews are one of the most visible pieces.

Why reviews matter more for cleaners than for most businesses

A review is the closest thing a cleaning company has to a free sample. People cannot see your work before they hire you, so they look for proof that someone like them trusted you and was happy. For a homeowner, that proof lowers the fear of a no-show or a damaged item. For an office manager, it answers the question of whether you will be on time at six in the morning.

Reviews also feed your map visibility. Recent reviews that mention the type of job and the neighbourhood give Google more to work with when someone searches for a cleaner nearby. That is one reason reviews sit next to a well set up profile and good local SEO as a core part of getting found.

Reviews will not rescue a weak service. What they do is make a good service visible. If your crews do careful work and your customers are quietly happy, reviews are how you turn that quiet happiness into new bookings.

What a useful review looks like

The best reviews are specific. They name the kind of job, the person who did it and what the customer noticed. You cannot script this, but you can nudge it with a simple question when you ask, which we cover below.

Tidy your profile before you ask for reviews

When a customer taps your review link, they land on your listing, and they often read it before writing anything. If it looks half finished, some will close the page. Spend an hour first making sure your business name matches what is on your van and website, your phone number works, your hours are right and your service area lists the places you really work.

Add real photos too. Finished kitchens, tidy offices, your crew in branded shirts and your equipment all help. Avoid stock images, since they tell a visitor nothing about who will turn up at the door. A customer who sees a friendly, real team is more likely to finish a review and more likely to say something personal in it.

List your services with short, plain descriptions, and pick the category that fits your biggest line of work. None of this is glamorous, but it means the people you send to your profile arrive at something that looks cared for. Think of it as tidying the shop before the customers walk in.

A quick pre-ask check

  • Business name, phone number and website match everywhere
  • Service area lists real places, not every town nearby
  • At least a handful of recent, real photos
  • Services listed with plain descriptions
  • Old reviews have replies, even short ones

Fix any gaps before you start the routine, so the first new reviews land on a profile that deserves them.

Find your review link step by step on Google.ca

Most Canadian customers will reach your listing through Google.ca on a phone, so do this setup the way they will see it. Sign in to the Google account that manages your Business Profile, then search for your business name on Google.ca. Your listing should appear with a set of management options above it.

  1. Search for your exact business name while signed in as the profile owner or manager.
  2. Choose the option to ask for reviews or share a review form.
  3. Copy the link and paste it into a note you can reach from your phone and your office computer.
  4. Open the link in a private window to confirm it lands on the review box for the right business.
  5. Shorten it with a QR code generator or a simple link tool if you plan to print it, and scan the result before you print.

If you serve customers in a few cities, such as Hamilton, Mississauga and Toronto, there is still only one listing and one link. Do not set up extra listings for each city just to collect more reviews, since duplicate or fake locations can get a profile suspended. Our guide on city pages without copy and paste shows the safe way to speak to each area on your own site.

Get your direct review link and test it

Every extra tap loses people. Before you ask anyone for anything, find your direct review link. In your Google Business Profile, look for the option to get more reviews or share a review form. Copy the link it gives you. That one link opens the review box straight away, so the customer does not have to search for your business.

Then test it properly. Open it on your own phone, once while signed in and once in a private window. Try it on a second phone if you can. Many customers are signed in to Google on their phone already, which makes it quick. Others will need to sign in first, and that is fine as long as the link still lands them in the right place.

Save the link somewhere your whole team can find it, such as a pinned note or a shared document. If the link lives only on your phone, the routine will break the first time you are on holiday.

Add a QR code for the physical world

A QR code that points to the same link works well on a printed card, a quote sheet or a leave-behind on the kitchen counter. Make the code large enough to scan easily and put a short line next to it, such as asking for a quick note about how the clean went. Check it scans before you print a batch.

Ask when the customer is happiest

The best moment to ask is right after the job, when the space looks great and the customer has just said thank you. Many cleaners wait a week and the feeling has faded. Others never ask at all because it feels awkward. Building the ask into the end of every visit takes the awkwardness out, because it becomes part of how you finish a job, not a favour you are begging for.

Timing differs a little by service. For a one-time move-out clean, send the message the same afternoon, when the landlord or the new owner has just seen the place. For a regular house clean, wait until after the third or fourth visit, once the customer has a feel for your work. For commercial clients, ask the person who actually deals with you day to day, not just the accounts contact.

A short worked example

Picture a small team that does move-out cleans in a mid-sized Canadian city. The owner used to send one message a week, in a batch, on Friday. Customers from Monday had mostly forgotten the details by then. She changed to sending the message from her phone within two hours of finishing each job, using a saved snippet. Replies became more specific, because the customer still remembered the oven and the baseboards. This is a made-up scenario, but the lesson is real: ask while it is fresh.

Guide customers toward specifics without scripting them

A review that says great job is nice, but it does little for the next reader. A review that says the crew cleaned a rental after a long tenancy and left the oven spotless tells a story. You cannot write it for the customer, and you must not. What you can do is ask a simple, open question that helps them remember what stood out.

For example, you might end your message with a line asking what they liked most about the clean, or whether anything surprised them. That small prompt tends to pull out details like punctuality, the crew's manner, the care taken with pets or the way a tricky stain was handled. It invites an honest answer without telling them what to say or how many stars to give.

Avoid anything that sounds like a script. Do not offer sample wording, do not suggest keywords and do not ask them to mention your city. If a customer happens to write the neighbourhood and the service, that is a bonus, not something to engineer. Readers can spot a manufactured review quickly.

Open questions that work

  • What stood out most about the clean?
  • Was there anything we did that you did not expect?
  • How did the crew treat your home or workspace?
  • Would you tell a friend or a colleague about us, and why?

Pick one, not all four. A single easy question is a gentle nudge. A list of questions feels like homework, and homework does not get finished.

Sample review-request texts you can copy

Keep the message short, warm and in your own voice. Use the customer's first name, mention the cleaner or the job, and put the link at the end. Here are three examples for different situations. Treat them as starting points and change the words so they sound like you.

Example: after a regular home clean

Hi Priya, it's Dana from Maple Row Cleaning. Thanks for having us in today. Jess said the kitchen turned out really well. If you have a minute, would you mind sharing how it went on Google? It helps other families find us. Here's the link: [your review link]. Thanks again!

Example: after a move-out clean

Hi Marcus, Dana here from Maple Row Cleaning. The place is all done and ready for the walk-through. If you were happy with the work, a short Google review would mean a lot to a small local business. Here's the link: [your review link]. Good luck with the move!

Example: a gentle reminder two or three days later

Hi Priya, just a quick nudge in case this got buried. If you have a minute to leave a Google review about Tuesday's clean, here's the link: [your review link]. No pressure at all, and thank you either way.

Notice what these do not do. They do not promise a discount, they do not ask for five stars and they do not hint at a particular answer. They simply ask for an honest note. That keeps you inside Google's rules and tends to produce more believable reviews. Asking in French and English for Quebec and bilingual customers

If you work in Quebec, Ottawa, Gatineau, Moncton or anywhere with a large French speaking population, send the request in the customer's language. A customer who booked in French will usually feel more comfortable leaving a review in French, and a French message shows respect. Google shows reviews in whatever language they were written in, so there is no need to worry about mixing.

Keep the French simple and natural, the way you would talk to a customer, and have a French speaker read it before you save it. A stiff, literal translation does more harm than good. If you serve a bilingual area, ask what language the customer prefers when they book, and note it in your booking system so the right template goes out every time.

Example: a French request after a home clean

Bonjour Marie-Eve, c'est Dana de Maple Row Cleaning. Merci de nous avoir ouvert votre porte aujourd'hui. Si vous avez une minute, pourriez-vous partager votre expérience sur Google? Cela aide d'autres familles à nous trouver. Voici le lien : [your review link]. Merci encore!

Email, invoice and printed card versions of the ask

Not every customer lives on text. Some clients, especially in offices and condos, prefer email, and many older customers are happier with something printed. Having a version for each channel means you can meet people where they are, and it gives you a second chance with anyone who missed the text.

An email can be a bit longer than a text, but keep it brief. Put the link on its own line so it is easy to tap, and add a plain sign-off from the person who dealt with the customer. If you send invoices by email, a single line near the bottom with the review link works well and costs nothing.

Example: a short thank you email

Subject: Thanks from Maple Row Cleaning

Hi Priya, thank you for having us in this week. We hope the house feels fresh. If you're happy with the work, would you share a quick review on Google? It helps small local businesses like ours. Here's the link: [your review link]. If anything wasn't right, just reply to this email and I'll sort it out. Dana

Example: a line for a printed card or invoice

Happy with your clean? A quick Google review helps our small team. Scan the code or visit the link below. If something wasn't right, call us first at [your number] and we'll fix it.

That last sentence does two jobs. It invites happy customers to review, and it gives unhappy ones a direct route to you before they post. Both outcomes are good for your business.

Build a simple three step routine

You do not need software to start, though a booking system that sends messages automatically helps once you are busy. What you do need is a routine that happens every time without someone having to remember. Here is a plain version that works for most small and mid-sized cleaning companies.

  1. Finish the job and let the customer see the result. Say thank you in person if they are home.
  2. Within a couple of hours, send the thank you text with the direct review link.
  3. If there is no reply after two or three days, send one short reminder. Then stop.
  4. For recurring clients, ask again once or twice a year, since their experience has grown.
  5. Log who you asked and who replied, even in a simple spreadsheet, so you can see what is working.

Stopping after the reminder matters. Customers who ignore two messages have given you an answer, and a third one tips from friendly into irritating. Respect that and move on.

Timing windows and reminder cadence

It helps to have exact rules, so nobody on your team has to guess. Use these as defaults and adjust them once you see what your customers do. The first message goes out within two hours of finishing the job, because that is when the result is clearest in the customer's mind. The reminder goes out two to three days later, ideally at a time of day when people are on their phones, such as early evening.

Send nothing late at night or very early in the morning, and avoid statutory holidays and long weekends when messages tend to get buried. For recurring clients, wait until the third or fourth visit before the first ask, then no more than once or twice a year. For one-time jobs, you only ever get one shot and one reminder, so make the first message count.

Example: an in-person script for the end of a visit

Crew lead: We're all finished here. Have a look around and tell us if anything needs another pass. Customer: It looks great, thank you. Crew lead: That's good to hear. If you have a minute later, we'd really appreciate a short Google review. I'll send the link by text in a bit, and there's a card on the counter with a code too. Thanks for having us.

That script takes about fifteen seconds, and it never pressures. The crew lead checks for problems first, then asks. That order matters, because it keeps a fixable complaint from turning into a public one.

Train your crew to mention it naturally

Your cleaners see the customer's reaction first. A customer who says the bathroom has never looked this good is telling your crew member something worth passing on. Give each person one easy sentence to use in that moment, such as asking whether the customer would mind sharing that on Google, followed by a mention that a message with the link is on its way.

Resist the urge to turn this into a contest. Paying staff per review, or running a leaderboard, tends to create pressure. Crews start steering customers, rushing the ask or hinting at what to write, and the reviews come out stiff or uneven. A relaxed mention from the person who did the work does far more than a quota.

It does help to share good reviews with the team. When a customer names a cleaner in a review, read it out at the next team meeting or send it in the group chat. Recognition costs nothing, and it shows the crew that careful work gets noticed.

What to put in a one page crew guide

  • The one sentence to use when a customer is pleased
  • Where to find the review link and QR card
  • What never to say, such as offering a discount for a review
  • What to do if a customer mentions a problem instead
  • Who to tell, so the office can follow up quickly

That last item is important. If a customer mentions a problem, the right move is to fix it quietly before any review is written, not to steer them away from reviewing.

Asking commercial clients and property managers

Office and building clients behave differently from homeowners. The person who signs your contract may never see your crew, and the person who sees your crew may not feel it is their place to post online for the company. A plain, polite email to the right contact usually works better than a text, and it is fine to ask for a review after a quarterly check-in or a contract renewal, when the relationship is clearly going well.

For condo boards and property managers, a review that mentions the type of building and the kind of work, such as common areas or post-construction cleaning, is valuable to the next manager who is searching. Our guide to condo cleaning marketing covers how to present that work, and our page on commercial and janitorial marketing goes further on the business side.

Example: an email to a property manager

Hi Sam, thanks for the smooth quarter at the building. If you're comfortable with it, would you share a few words about our service on Google? It helps other managers in the area see what to expect. Here's the link: [your review link]. Happy to adjust anything on our side too.

Some clients have policies that stop staff posting reviews for suppliers. If you hear that, accept it graciously. You can still ask whether a short written reference you could use, with their permission, on your website would be possible.

Use the seasons, but never fake a rush

Cleaning demand moves through the year in Canada, and so do your best moments to ask. Spring cleaning brings a wave of one-time deep cleans. The weeks around the start of summer and September are heavy for move-outs. After a long winter, window and carpet work picks up, and the weeks before the holidays bring a rush of homes being prepared for guests.

Busy periods mean you finish more jobs, so they are the best time to collect reviews, as long as the routine does not slip. The risk is that you get so stretched that nobody sends the messages. Decide before the busy season starts who is responsible and make the messages automatic or saved as templates, so the routine survives your busiest weeks.

Quiet periods are a good time to do the catch-up work: reply to old reviews, refresh photos and send a polite note to recurring customers who have never reviewed. Our seasonal marketing calendar for Canadian cleaning companies shows how to plan around these peaks. Whatever the season, keep the ask honest and never bunch reviews unnaturally just to look busy.

How to reply to good reviews, with templates

A reply is a public message to every future customer who reads the page. It shows how you treat people. Reply to every review where you can, and do it within a day or two. A warm, specific reply tells readers someone is paying attention, and it gives you a natural place to mention the type of service and the area without stuffing keywords.

Keep it short. Use the customer's first name, thank them for something specific, mention the service and sign off with a real name. Avoid copying the same line onto every review, since readers notice and it sounds robotic. Here are two templates you can adapt.

Thanks so much, Priya. Jess will be glad to hear the kitchen turned out well. We love helping busy families get their weekends back. See you next visit! Dana, Maple Row Cleaning
Thank you, Marcus, and congratulations on the new place. We're happy the move-out clean made your walk-through easy. If you ever need a hand at the new address, you know where we are. Dana

If a review is just stars with no text, still reply. A short thank you is plenty. It shows you care about the customer, and it costs you thirty seconds.

How to reply to a bad review, with a template

A bad review hurts, especially when you work hard and care about your customers. The reply is not really for the reviewer. It is for the next person reading, who is deciding whether to trust you. A calm, factual reply can win you more trust than a page of perfect reviews, because it shows what happens when something goes wrong.

Aim for four things: acknowledge the problem, apologise for the experience without grovelling, say what you are doing about it and move the conversation offline. Do not argue, do not blame the customer, and do not share private details of the job. Here is a template you can adjust.

Hi Alex, thank you for telling us, and I'm sorry the clean did not meet your expectations. That is not the standard we aim for. I'd like to understand what happened and make it right. Please call me directly at [your number] or email [your email], and we'll arrange a return visit. Dana, Owner

Read it back before you post. If it sounds defensive, rewrite it. If you are still angry, wait an hour. A reply written in a rush is the one that gets screenshotted.

A step by step plan for handling a bad review

When a bad review lands, follow the same steps every time. Having a process stops you reacting emotionally and gives your team something to follow when you are not around.

  1. Pause. Do not reply for at least an hour. Read the review twice.
  2. Check your records. Find the booking, the crew notes, any photos and any messages with the customer.
  3. Decide whether it is fair. Many complaints are partly right, even when the tone stings.
  4. Contact the customer privately first, by phone or message, if you have their details. Offer to fix the problem.
  5. Post a short public reply using the template above, with no excuses and no private details.
  6. Fix the cause. If a crew missed something, coach them. If a process failed, change it.
  7. If the customer is happy after the fix, you may politely ask whether they would update the review. Never pressure them or offer a reward.
  8. If the review breaks Google's rules, flag it and keep your public reply polite in the meantime.

Over time, patterns show up. If three reviews in a row mention the same missed spot, the fix is a checklist change, not a better reply. Treat bad reviews as free feedback, even when it stings.

If you cannot tell whether a review is fair, ask someone outside the business to read it with you. A fresh pair of eyes sees the tone you cannot.

A worked example: a mixed review and how to handle it

Here is a made-up scenario to show the steps in action. A small team cleans a customer's townhouse. The customer leaves a three star review saying the kitchen was great, but the crew arrived forty minutes late and nobody called. It is not a disaster, but it is the kind of review that makes a future customer hesitate about punctuality.

The owner reads it twice and checks the booking notes. A tyre had gone flat, and the crew lead had meant to call but got caught up. The complaint is fair. She phones the customer, apologises for the lack of a call and offers a small gesture that is not tied to the review, in the form of a priority slot next time. Then she posts a public reply.

Hi Jordan, thank you for the honest feedback. We should have called as soon as we knew we would be late, and I'm sorry we did not. We have changed our process so crews call ahead if they are running behind. I'm glad the kitchen turned out well, and I appreciate you telling us. Dana

Notice the shape. The owner agrees with the fair part, names the fix and credits the good part. She does not make excuses and she does not quote the flat tyre. Readers see a business that listens. Later, the crew gets a simple rule: call if you are more than ten minutes behind.

Fake reviews, unfair reviews and what Google does not allow

Some negative reviews are not from real customers. A competitor, a former employee or someone who never booked you can leave one. If a review clearly does not match any job you did, or contains abusive or off-topic content, you can flag it through your profile. Removal is never guaranteed and can take time, so reply politely and factually while you wait, for example by saying you cannot find a record of that visit and inviting them to get in touch.

Just as important, keep your own side clean. Google does not allow buying reviews, offering rewards or discounts for positive ones, asking only the customers you expect to be happy while quietly skipping the rest, or having staff or family write reviews for the company. Posting reviews on a customer's behalf is also risky. Reviews that get removed can set you back further than never having had them.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Offering a discount or gift in return for a review
  • Using a tablet in the office so every review comes from one device
  • Asking only your happiest customers
  • Writing reviews yourself or asking friends who never used the service
  • Arguing with a reviewer in public
  • Ignoring a review for weeks

If your profile ever gets flagged or suspended, start by reading Google's current guidelines and fixing what they point to. Our guide on setting up your service area also covers common profile mistakes that can cause trouble.

Your review routine checklist

A routine only works if it is small enough to keep doing. Print this list, stick it in the office and give it to whoever runs your admin. Tick it off weekly and monthly, and you will keep a steady flow of honest reviews without ever having to push.

After every job

  • Send the thank you text or email with the direct review link within a couple of hours
  • Note in your log who you asked
  • Pass any customer problem to the owner the same day

Every week

  • Send one gentle reminder to anyone who has not replied after two or three days
  • Reply to every new review, good or bad
  • Read the reviews aloud to the crew, especially ones that name a cleaner

Every month

  • Test the review link and QR code again on a phone
  • Look for repeated themes in complaints and fix the cause
  • Add one or two new quotes to the right pages on your website
  • Check that your profile details, photos and services are still correct

Metrics to track and how much time it takes

You can run this routine in under an hour a week for a small team, once the templates are saved. Most of that time is replying to reviews and checking that last week's requests went out. Block the time on a fixed day, such as Friday morning, so it does not slide.

Track only a few numbers, and write them down in a simple sheet. Review count is the obvious one, but the more useful ones are how many requests you sent, how many of those became reviews and how quickly you replied. The share of requests that turn into reviews is your response rate. If it is low, look at your message, your timing and the link before changing anything else.

Also watch what happens next. Note how many calls and quote requests mention reading your reviews, and ask new customers how they found you. That gives you a rough sense of whether reviews turn into work, which is the conversion that matters. You do not need a dashboard for this. A short note each month is enough to spot a trend and decide whether to keep going, change the message or ask your crews to mention it more.

Your first thirty days: an action plan

Reading a guide is easy and acting on it is harder, so here is a simple plan. Do not try to do everything at once. Treat each week as one small job, and by the end of the month you will have a routine running.

  1. Week one: tidy your profile, find your direct review link, test it on two phones and save it where the whole team can reach it.
  2. Week two: write your saved text and email templates, order or print a few cards with a QR code and brief your crews on the one sentence they will use.
  3. Week three: start sending requests after every job and log who you asked. Reply to every existing review, including old ones.
  4. Week four: send gentle reminders to anyone who has not replied, read what came in and look for patterns, good and bad.

After thirty days, review the log. Which message got the most replies? Which jobs produced the best feedback? Keep what worked and drop what did not. Then repeat the routine every month, with the checklist above as your guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many Google reviews does a cleaning company need?

There is no magic number. A steady trickle of genuine reviews that mention the service and area matters more than a sudden burst. Aim to add a few each month as part of your normal routine, and focus on recent, specific feedback. A new company with a handful of honest reviews can look more trustworthy than an older one with a long gap since the last review.

Is it okay to ask customers for Google reviews?

Yes. Google allows businesses to ask. What it does not allow is paying for reviews, offering rewards or discounts, or selectively asking only customers you expect to be happy. Ask everyone, keep the request friendly and low pressure, and accept whatever honest feedback comes back, including the occasional critical one.

When is the best time to send a review request?

Within a couple of hours of finishing the job, while the customer can still see the result and remember the experience. For recurring cleans, wait until after the third or fourth visit. For commercial clients, a quarterly check-in or contract renewal works well. One gentle reminder a few days later is fine, but do not send a third message.

Should I reply to every Google review?

Ideally yes. Replying shows future customers that you pay attention, and it gives you a natural place to mention the type of service and area without stuffing keywords. Keep replies short, warm and specific, use the customer's first name and avoid pasting the same text on every review. Even a plain star rating with no text deserves a quick thank you.

Can I ask a customer to change or remove a bad review?

You can fix the problem first and, if the customer is happy afterwards, politely ask whether they would consider updating their review. Never pressure them, never offer a reward and never ask more than once. Many people will not change it, and that is fine. A calm public reply that shows you made things right still helps.

What should I do about a fake or unfair review?

Flag it through your Google Business Profile if it breaks the rules, for example if it is spam, abusive or from someone who was never your customer. Removal is not guaranteed and can take a while. In the meantime, reply politely and factually, say you cannot find a record of the visit and invite them to contact you directly.

Can I show Google reviews on my own website?

Yes, with care. Quote customers accurately, keep the wording close to what they wrote and ask permission before using their full name outside Google. Place short quotes next to the relevant service page and link to your full listing. If you add review markup, follow Google's current guidelines and only mark up reviews that genuinely exist.

How long does it take to see results from more reviews?

Reviews build slowly. Many owners notice better trust and more calls from people who have already read about them, before they see any change in map visibility. Visibility may improve over weeks and months, but nobody can promise a ranking. The honest approach is to keep the routine going and treat reviews as a long-term habit.
