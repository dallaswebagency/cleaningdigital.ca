Residential Cleaning

Condo Cleaning Marketing: Win Work in Towers

By Nakul Chandra, Founder and Lead Strategist · November 11, 2026 · 12 min read

Book a callEmail us

Walk through the downtown core of Toronto, Vancouver, Calgary or Montreal and you will see towers in every direction. Behind those lobby doors is a steady stream of cleaning work, and a good share of it goes to companies that simply understand how buildings run. The cleaning looks like house cleaning from the outside. The customer, the access rules and the way people search are all different.

If your website treats a condo like any other home, you are leaving your best story untold. Owners, investors, short-term rental hosts and property managers each want something slightly different, and they can tell within seconds whether you have done this before.

This guide walks through how to market a cleaning business to condo buyers, from page structure and access questions to turnover checklists and a sample message for property managers. Take what fits your business and leave the rest.

Why condo cleaning is its own market

A house has a driveway, a front door and usually one decision maker. A condo has an elevator booking, a concierge, a board with rules, and sometimes a landlord who lives in another province. The cleaning itself may be similar, but everything around it is different, and customers know it.

That difference is your opening. Many cleaning companies write one generic residential page and hope it covers everything. If you build a page that speaks directly to tower living, you match what condo owners actually type into Google, and you sound like someone who has solved their particular headaches before.

It also changes the pricing conversation. When a customer sees that you already understand move-in bookings, loading dock windows and key fob returns, they stop comparing you only on hours and rates. They start comparing you on how little stress you add to their week.

Know the four buyers in a condo building

Condo work rarely comes from one type of customer. Getting clear on who you want helps you write pages that convince rather than hedge.

  • Owner occupiers who want a regular weekly or biweekly clean and care most about trust and consistency.
  • Investors with rental units who need a thorough clean between tenants and a simple booking process.
  • Short-term rental hosts who need fast turnovers, linens handled properly and honest reporting if something is damaged.
  • Property managers who may hire for vacant suites, post-repair cleans or common areas.

You do not have to serve all four. A company that only does turnovers can build a solid business, and so can one that only does recurring owner cleans. What does not work is a page that tries to talk to everyone in the same breath.

Pick your top one or two buyers, write to them first, and mention the others lower down the page. You can always add dedicated pages later as that part of the business grows.

What condo customers worry about before they book

Most house customers worry about whether the cleaner will do a good job. Condo customers worry about that too, but they carry a second set of fears that has nothing to do with dust. They worry that your team will break a building rule and that the owner will get the call or the fine.

Think about it from their side. A condo owner may have signed a form promising the board that any contractor will follow quiet hours, book the elevator and use the right entrance. If your team gets it wrong, the owner is the one answering to the property manager, not you.

So the best condo marketing does something simple. It names those worries out loud and shows that you have a calm, boring process for each one. Booking the elevator, signing in, parking in the right spot, returning the fob. Boring is exactly what a nervous owner wants to read.

Spend a few minutes listing every building problem you have run into over the past year. Each one is a sentence for your page and a point for your quote form.

A condo cleaning page outline checklist

Give condo cleaning its own page instead of burying it in a long services list. It should answer the questions a nervous owner or a busy manager will have within the first screen: what you clean, how you handle the building and how to ask for a quote. Use this outline when you plan the page.

  • A plain headline that names condo cleaning and your city, such as condo cleaning in Mississauga.
  • Two or three sentences on who you help and which unit types you clean.
  • A short section on elevator bookings, concierge sign-in and key or fob handling.
  • A clear list of what is included and what is extra, without quoting numbers.
  • Real photos of finished condo rooms, used with the owner's permission.
  • Proof of insurance and a line on how your staff are screened.
  • A short quote form that asks for building name, unit size and preferred dates.
  • A few honest reviews that mention condos or buildings.

This is the kind of structure our website design work builds into service pages, so every page has one purpose and one obvious next step.

Write the headline and first screen for a nervous reader

The top of your condo page does most of the selling. A visitor decides in a few seconds whether you understand their situation. Skip the vague slogans about sparkling results and say what you actually do, for whom and where.

A headline like condo cleaning for owners, hosts and property managers in downtown Calgary tells people they are in the right place. Under it, add one short paragraph that names the things you handle: elevator bookings, sign-in, key handling and building rules. Then put a clear button for a quote or a call.

Resist the urge to pack the first screen with every service you offer. Condo visitors are often on a phone, in a lobby or between meetings. They want to know quickly whether you serve their building and how to reach you.

Read your first screen out loud. If it could belong to any cleaning company in any city, rewrite it until it could only belong to yours.

Explain building access before they ask

The biggest worry for a condo customer is not the dust. It is the call from the property manager saying your team parked in the wrong spot or used the passenger elevator during a move-in. If your page speaks to those worries, you have already answered the objection that stops many people from booking.

You do not need an essay. A short section with a few plain sentences is enough. Say that your team books the service elevator when the building requires it, signs in at the front desk, follows quiet hours and returns fobs or keys the same day. Say that you ask for building rules before the first visit and that you do not start work until access is confirmed.

Only describe things you actually do. If you are still building this process, build it first and write about it afterward. Customers forgive a small company for being new, but they do not forgive a promise that was never kept. A consistent access story also makes your Google Business Profile posts and photos feel like they belong with the website.

Building access questions to ask before you quote

A quote given without access details is a guess, and guesses cost you money or goodwill. Before you price a condo job, collect the answers below. You can turn them into fields on your quote form or ask them on the phone.

  1. What is the building name and address, and is there a concierge or front desk?
  2. Does the elevator need to be booked, how far ahead, and does the building charge the owner a deposit or fee?
  3. Where can the team park, and is there a visitor or loading zone with a time limit?
  4. How will we get in: lockbox, fob, key from the concierge, or the owner meeting us?
  5. Are there quiet hours or restricted days for contractors?
  6. Can we bring a vacuum, mop and supplies through the lobby, or must we use a service entrance?
  7. Where do garbage and recycling go, and are there rules for bagging or disposal?
  8. Does the unit have in-suite laundry, a balcony, or surfaces that need special care, such as natural stone counters?

Write the answers on the job card so the next team member does not have to ask again. It also gives you a record if a question about building rules comes up later.

Design a quote form that collects what you need

Your form is where a curious visitor turns into a real lead, and it is also where many cleaning websites lose people. Too many fields and visitors give up. Too few and you spend your evening chasing details. Aim for the middle, and ask for the things that change your plan.

Here is a simple way to build the form step by step.

  1. Start with name, phone and email, so you can always reply.
  2. Ask for the building name and address, since this tells you which rules apply.
  3. Ask for the unit size and number of bathrooms in plain language.
  4. Ask what kind of service they want: regular clean, one-time deep clean, turnover or move-out.
  5. Ask for preferred dates and whether the elevator is already booked.
  6. Add one open box for anything else we should know.

Keep the confirmation message friendly and specific. Tell people when you will reply and what happens next. A good confirmation makes the person feel looked after before you have even spoken.

Position recurring owner cleans and turnovers differently

Recurring cleans and turnovers are different products, and they deserve different wording. A recurring client wants the same friendly team, the same routine and a service that fades into the background. A turnover client wants a unit ready by a hard deadline and a clear report when it is done.

On your page, describe each one separately. For recurring cleans, talk about consistency, a regular schedule and how you handle requests such as extra windows or fridge cleans. For turnovers, talk about speed, checklists, supply restocking and how you report problems.

This separation also helps you plan your week. Turnovers cluster around weekends, month ends and holiday periods, while recurring work fills the quieter days. Knowing the mix lets you hire and schedule with fewer surprises.

If you offer both, link them to each other so a host who also owns a home can find the residential side, and a long-term owner can find the one-time services they may need later.

A turnover cleaning checklist for short-term rentals

Hosts and investors want one thing above all: a unit that is ready on time and looks like the photos. A written checklist, shared with the host, builds trust and keeps your team consistent. Here is a starter list you can adapt. Adjust it with each host, since every property has its own standards.

  • Strip beds, bag used linens and remake with fresh sets.
  • Clean and sanitize bathrooms, including mirrors, fixtures, grout and the shower drain.
  • Wipe kitchen counters, appliance fronts, the inside of the microwave and fridge shelves.
  • Check and restock soap, paper goods, coffee and other supplies the host has agreed to.
  • Vacuum and mop all floors, including under beds where reachable.
  • Check for damage, stains or missing items and photograph anything unusual.
  • Empty garbage and recycling, reset the thermostat and lock up as instructed.

Many cleaners also add a short photo set at the end of each job, sent to the host. It saves a lot of messages and gives both sides a clear record if a guest later reports a problem.

Win short-term rental hosts without overpromising

Hosts talk to each other, and reliability is how a turnover cleaner earns referrals. They also book outside business hours, which is why a simple booking path matters. Our guide to online booking shows how a host can reserve a slot without waiting for a callback.

On your page and in your messages, be direct about what you can and cannot do. Can you handle a same-day turnover when a guest checks out late? Do you work on holidays? What happens if a unit is far dirtier than expected? Hosts would rather hear a clear limit than find out on a busy Saturday.

Here is a short example reply you could send to a host who has just enquired. It is only an example, so use your own words.

Hi Jordan, thanks for getting in touch. We do turnovers for several suites in the area and send a short photo set once each unit is done. Could you tell me the building, the checkout and check-in times, and whether linens are on site or supplied? Once I have that, I can confirm if our team can fit your schedule.

It is polite, short and asks only for what you need to give a real answer.

Earn trust with property managers

Property managers influence many units at once, so one good relationship can bring steady work. They also remember who caused them trouble. They are usually busy and looking for a vendor who is easy to reach, properly insured and quick to respond.

Make it simple for them to try you. A short, professional page or PDF with your insurance details, how your staff are screened, your service area and your contact information goes a long way. Offer references on request, and never claim building approvals or relationships you do not actually hold.

If you work in one building and do well, ask the manager whether they would be comfortable recommending you to colleagues at other properties. A warm introduction beats a cold email every time. Our guide to questions to ask before hiring a marketing agency is useful when you want to decide how much of this outreach to hand off.

Managers also care about common-area and post-construction work, so mention it if you offer it. If you do not, say so plainly and focus on what you do well.

A sample intro message to a property manager

Cold outreach to a property manager works best when it is short, specific and easy to answer. The example below is only a starting point. Replace the details with real ones about your business and their building, and do not send the same text to everyone.

Hi Maria, I am Sam from Northgate Cleaning. We clean condo suites in the downtown area, and I noticed your building handles a lot of turnovers. If you ever need a reliable team for a vacant suite or a post-repair clean, I would be glad to send our insurance details and a short summary of how we handle elevator bookings and sign-in. No pressure at all. Happy to answer questions by email or phone.

Notice what is missing: no big claims, no long list of services and no pushy deadline. It tells the manager who you are, what you do and what you will send if they are interested.

If you do not hear back, one polite follow-up after a week or two is plenty. Something like a quick note asking whether the first message reached the right person works well. After that, let it rest and try again when you have a new reason to write.

Target the neighbourhoods where towers cluster

Condos group in particular parts of each city: downtown cores, waterfront districts, transit corridors and newer growth areas. In Vancouver that might be Yaletown and Coal Harbour. In Calgary it could be the Beltline. In Toronto it is the waterfront and the areas along the main subway lines. If you serve those areas, name them honestly on the page.

Do not list places you do not work. It is tempting to add every neighbourhood to catch more searches, but it can confuse customers and your map listing. Our guide to the Google Business Profile service area shows how to set this up without overreaching.

If you want separate pages for separate areas, make each one genuinely different. Mention real building types, real access quirks and real photos from that part of town. Our piece on city pages without copy and paste explains how to avoid pages that all say the same thing with a place name swapped in.

Use photos that look like real condo work

Condo customers want to picture their own suite. A photo of a bright, clean kitchen with a city view says more than a stock image of a smiling cleaner holding a mop. Take real photos, with permission, and avoid anything that shows personal items, documents or the building name if the owner would rather you did not.

Good condo photos are simple. A made bed in a small bedroom. A bathroom with clean glass and a dry counter. A kitchen with clear surfaces. Take them in natural light, from the doorway, so the room looks like it really does.

If you cannot get customer permission, photograph your own supplies, your team in uniform outside the building or the equipment you bring for elevator-friendly work. Anything real beats a polished image that could belong to any company.

Add short, honest captions. Something like a one-bedroom turnover finished before check-in gives visitors a clue about the kind of work you do and the timing you can meet.

Collect reviews that mention condos and buildings

Reviews matter just as much as photos. Ask condo customers to mention the type of home and what you did well, such as handling a move-out clean or arriving on time for a tight turnover. Do not write reviews for them and do not offer rewards in return.

The best moment to ask is right after a smooth job, when the owner or host is relieved and the result is fresh. A short text with a direct link is usually enough. Our guide to getting more Google reviews gives a simple routine for asking at the right moment.

A handful of specific reviews on the condo page will do more than a long list of generic praise. Show the ones that mention buildings, elevators, timing or communication. Reply to every review, including the average ones, in a calm and friendly way. Future customers read the replies as closely as the reviews.

Set up your Google profile and page markup for condo searches

Many condo owners find cleaners on the map first. Make sure your profile lists condo cleaning as a service, uses a service area that matches where you actually work and links to the condo page rather than only the home page. Add photos that match the page and keep your hours accurate.

Behind the scenes, structured data helps search engines understand what your page is about. Our guide to schema markup for cleaning companies explains the basics without the jargon, and your developer can add it in a short session.

Do not expect markup to fix a weak page. It supports a page that already answers real questions. The same goes for the FAQ at the bottom of your condo page: write it from the questions customers really ask, such as elevator booking, key handling and what to do if the building has strict hours.

When the page, the profile and the reviews all tell the same story, searchers get a consistent picture of who you are.

Plan around the condo calendar

Condo demand follows the calendar more than most people realise. Moving seasons in many Canadian cities bring a burst of move-in and move-out cleans, often around the end of the month. Summer is busy for short-term rentals in tourist cities, while winter brings salt, slush and lobby traffic that affect how owners think about floors and entry mats.

Plan your marketing a few weeks ahead of those periods. Update the condo page with a short note about booking early for month-end moves. Post a few photos on your profile. Let existing property manager contacts know when your calendar opens up.

Our seasonal marketing calendar gives a broader view of how Canadian demand shifts through the year. For move-based work, the pillar page on move-in and move-out cleaning marketing is also worth a read.

In bilingual markets such as Montreal and Quebec City, make sure the condo page is available in French as well, written by a real French speaker rather than an automatic tool.

How boards, managers and corporations really decide

In a condo building the person who needs the cleaning is often not the person who hires you. For common areas and vacant units, the decision usually runs through a property manager, who reports to a board of directors, who answer to the owners. In some buildings the corporation itself holds the vendor list and the manager only recommends from it.

That means you are selling to a chain, not a single buyer. The manager wants someone reliable who creates no complaints. The board wants a vendor who is insured, fair and easy to explain to residents at the annual meeting. Owners just want the lobby to look good and their fees to stay sensible.

Before you pitch, find out who actually decides. A short, polite question to the front desk or the management office, such as who handles vendor enquiries for this building, saves weeks of writing to the wrong person.

Write your materials so each person in the chain can pass them along without editing. A one page summary a manager can forward to a board is worth more than a long brochure that nobody reads.

Bylaws, rules and vendor vetting

Every condo corporation has bylaws and house rules, and many have a vendor approval process on top. Contractors may need to register with the front desk, show proof of insurance, wear identification and follow set hours. Some buildings keep an approved vendor list, and getting onto it can take a few weeks of paperwork.

Treat that paperwork as a competitive advantage. Many small cleaners skip buildings that ask for forms, which leaves less competition for the ones who do the work. Keep a ready folder with your business details, insurance certificate, staff screening policy and a short description of your services.

When a manager sends you a vendor package, answer it promptly and completely. Slow or half finished paperwork tells them exactly how you will behave once you are working in their building.

Never ask your team to work around a rule because it seems silly. Quiet hours, elevator padding and sign-in sheets exist because someone once ignored them. Following the rules without complaint is one of the easiest ways to be remembered as the easy vendor.

Insurance, WSIB and the paperwork managers ask for

Property managers rarely hire a cleaner without seeing proof of cover. Expect to be asked for a certificate of insurance that names the building or corporation, and often proof that you are in good standing with your provincial workers compensation board. In Ontario that is WSIB, and other provinces have their own boards with their own rules.

Requirements differ between buildings and provinces, so ask your insurer and your workers compensation board what applies to you, rather than guessing. Do not describe coverage you do not hold, and do not let a certificate expire without noticing.

A simple document pack makes this painless.

  • Current certificate of insurance, ready to be reissued with a building named.
  • Proof of workers compensation coverage or clearance, where it applies to you.
  • A short description of how your staff are screened and trained.
  • Two or three references from managers or owners who agreed to be contacted.
  • A one page summary of your services and service area.

Having these in one folder means you can reply to a manager within the hour, which often decides who gets the first trial job.

RFPs, bids and how to respond without wasting a week

Larger buildings and management companies sometimes ask several cleaners for formal bids. It can feel like a lot of effort for work you may not win, but a well organised response is also a chance to look more professional than the competition.

Here is a straightforward way to handle a bid request.

  1. Read the whole request first and list every document it asks for.
  2. Ask for a site visit or a walkthrough, since you cannot price what you have not seen.
  3. Write down your assumptions, such as frequency, hours and what is included.
  4. Present your scope in plain language, with inclusions and exclusions clearly separated.
  5. Attach your insurance, references and staff screening summary.
  6. Send it before the deadline and confirm that it arrived.

Do not bid on everything. If the scope is vague, the building is far from your base or the timing does not fit your team, it is fine to decline politely. A short, courteous no keeps the door open for the next request.

Pricing models that work in condos, without the numbers

Condo work can be priced in several ways, and the right choice depends on the customer. Hourly pricing is simple but can make owners nervous about the clock. A flat price per unit size is easier to understand and works well for turnovers. A monthly contract suits common areas and recurring owner services.

Whatever model you choose, explain it clearly on the page without quoting numbers that will go out of date. Say what drives the price: unit size, condition, frequency, extras such as fridge or oven cleaning, and any building fees. Hosts and managers appreciate knowing the logic even before they see a quote.

Be careful with extras. Elevator deposits, parking charges and special disposal rules are the building's charges, not yours, so tell the customer who pays what before the first visit.

Finally, avoid pricing the first job so low that you cannot afford to do it properly. A rushed first impression in a small building travels fast, and so does a reputation for cutting corners.

Owner occupied units compared with rentals

An owner who lives in the unit and an investor who rents it out want different things, and your marketing should reflect that. The owner cares about trust, privacy and the feel of having someone in their home. The investor cares about speed, a checklist and a clear record.

For owners, talk about the same team, careful handling of belongings and flexible scheduling around work. Mention how you handle keys and alarms. For investors, talk about turnaround, standard checklists, photo reports and how you communicate with tenants or property managers.

It can help to show the difference side by side on your page, in two short columns or two short sections. Visitors then self select and land on the part that fits them.

Also think about who is at the door. In a rental, the tenant may be present or may have left items behind, while an owner may want to be home. Agree up front who will be there, and write it on the job card so the team arrives knowing what to expect.

Outreach timing and how often to follow up

Reaching out to managers and hosts is as much about timing as wording. Property managers are busiest at month end, around move cycles and during inspections, so a message on the last day of the month may sit unread. Mid-week, mid-morning tends to be calmer, though you know your own city and contacts best.

Plan a gentle rhythm. Send a first message. If there is no reply, follow up once after a week or two with something new, such as a recent photo set or a note that you have an opening in your calendar. After that, wait a few months before trying again.

Keep a simple list of who you contacted, when, and what they said. It stops you from repeating yourself and helps you notice patterns, such as which building types respond.

Here is a short follow-up you can adapt. It is an example only.

Hi Maria, just checking my earlier note reached you. We have a couple of openings for vacant suite cleans this month, and I am happy to send our insurance details if that helps. If now is not a good time, no problem at all.

Short, friendly and easy to ignore or answer. That is exactly the tone you want.

Talk to residents as well as managers

Even when a manager hires you, the residents are the ones who see your team in the lobby and the elevator. How they feel about you affects whether the manager keeps you. A friendly, tidy team in uniform, with a visible name badge and a polite greeting, does a lot of marketing without a word.

For work inside units, send a short confirmation message before each visit. Say who is coming, when they will arrive and which elevator and entrance they will use. If a delay happens, tell the resident early rather than after the fact.

For common areas, ask the manager whether a notice board message would help, such as telling residents when floors will be cleaned and which areas are wet. Residents appreciate knowing, and managers appreciate fewer complaints.

Treat every resident like a potential referral. Condo owners talk in elevators, group chats and building meetings. One calm, courteous interaction can quietly become the recommendation that brings your next suite.

Imagine a cleaner serving downtown Toronto condos. The towers are dense, many suites are rentals and elevator booking is common. The page leans on turnover work, access handling and quick quotes. The profile highlights the neighbourhoods served, and the manager outreach stresses insurance and a clear sign-in routine.

Now imagine a cleaner in Vancouver working around Yaletown and Coal Harbour. Many owners live in their units, strata rules matter and some buildings have strict move and contractor hours. Here the page leans more on recurring owner cleans, careful handling of belongings and respect for strata rules. Local wording matters too, since people there often say strata instead of condo corporation.

Your digital presence as a condo specialist

When a manager or a board member hears your name, the first thing they do is look you up. They will check your website, your Google listing and your reviews. If those three tell a consistent, professional story, you are already ahead of many cleaners.

Make sure your business name, phone number and address match everywhere. Add condo cleaning as a service on your profile. Keep photos recent. Reply to reviews. Make sure your website loads well on a phone, since many people check from a lobby or a meeting.

Do not forget the small things. A simple email address on your own domain looks more professional than a free email service when you send a bid. A clear About section with a real team photo shows managers who will actually be in their building.

None of this needs to be fancy. It just needs to be tidy and true, and to answer the questions a cautious manager will have before they ever pick up the phone.

A worked example: one cleaner, one tower, one page

Here is a hypothetical scenario to show how this fits together. Priya runs a small cleaning company in Ottawa. Most of her work is houses in the suburbs, but she has cleaned a few units in a nearby high-rise and enjoyed the work. She decides to build a condo page.

She writes a short section on elevator booking and sign-in, adds three photos taken with owner permission and lists the unit sizes she cleans. She builds a quote form that asks for the building name and whether the elevator is booked. She updates her Google profile with a condo cleaning service and links it to the new page.

Over the following months she notices that the forms from the condo page are more complete than her general enquiries. She has fewer back-and-forth messages and fewer surprises on the day. A couple of owners even mention the access section when they call.

None of this is magic. It is a page that answers real questions and a profile that points to it. The details here are invented for illustration, so your own results will depend on your city, your competition and how consistently you follow through.

Condo leads can come from search, the map listing, a referral or a manager's recommendation. Count calls and form submissions from the condo page separately so you can see what is working. Our guide to tracking calls and quote requests explains a simple way to do this.

Also ask every new customer how they found you, and write it down. After a few months you will know whether condos suit your business, which buildings produce repeat work and whether turnover jobs are more profitable than recurring cleans.

That tells you where to put your time, and whether more local SEO for condo searches makes sense. If you work in several cities, review each one on its own. Mistakes to avoid when marketing condo cleaning

Most condo marketing mistakes come from treating a building like a house. Avoid these and you will already be ahead of many competitors.

  • Using one generic residential page for every kind of customer.
  • Quoting before you know the elevator, parking and key rules.
  • Claiming building approvals or relationships you do not have.
  • Listing neighbourhoods you do not actually serve.
  • Using stock photos instead of real condo rooms.
  • Ignoring turnover hosts who book late at night or on weekends.
  • Forgetting to track which enquiries come from the condo page.
  • Sending property managers a long pitch with no insurance details.

Another quiet mistake is promising a turnaround you cannot keep. A host who loses a booking because a unit was not ready will not give you a second chance, and the story will travel through the building. Better to say no to a tight job than to say yes and fail.

Finally, do not copy a competitor's condo page. Customers can tell, search engines can tell, and you lose the one thing that makes you different.

A 30 day action plan

You do not need to rebuild your whole website. Work through this plan one week at a time and you will have a real condo presence by the end of the month.

  1. Week one: list the building problems you have faced and write the access section in plain language.
  2. Week two: draft the condo page using the outline above, add real photos and build the quote form.
  3. Week three: update your Google profile, link it to the page and ask recent condo customers for reviews.
  4. Week four: send a few personal messages to property managers and hosts, and set up tracking for the page.

After the month, look at what came in. Which messages got replies? Which form fields confused people? What did customers ask that the page did not answer? Fix those things first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a separate page for condo cleaning?

Usually yes. A dedicated page lets you explain building access, elevator bookings and unit sizes in language condo customers recognise. It also gives your Google profile a specific page to link to and matches the searches people actually make. If condos are only a tiny part of your work, a clear section on your residential page can be enough to start.

What should a condo cleaning quote form ask?

Ask for the building name, unit size, preferred dates, whether an elevator booking is needed and how you will get access. A few extra fields about pets, balconies and special surfaces also help. Keep the form short enough that people finish it. You can collect the rest by phone once you know the job is a fit.

How do I approach property managers without being pushy?

Keep the first message short, specific and easy to answer. Mention something real about their building, offer insurance details and a summary of how you handle access, and say you are happy to wait until they have a need. Follow up once politely. Never claim relationships or approvals you do not hold.

Is short-term rental cleaning worth offering?

It can be, if you can handle tight windows and different booking hours. Hosts value reliability, clear confirmation and honest reporting. Think about whether you have the staff and scheduling to meet short turnarounds before you advertise it. Start with a few hosts, learn the rhythm and expand only when your process is solid.

Should I list every condo building I clean in?

Only if the owners or managers are comfortable and it is genuinely helpful. Naming a neighbourhood is usually enough. Listing specific buildings can raise privacy concerns and may suggest an approval you do not have. If a manager is happy to be named as a reference, ask permission first and keep it accurate.

How do reviews help condo cleaning companies?

Reviews that mention condos, timing and communication reassure other owners and managers that you can handle building rules. Ask at a good moment, such as after a smooth turnover, and let customers write in their own words. Do not offer rewards or write reviews for them. A few specific reviews are worth more than many vague ones.

What insurance information should I share with a building?

Many buildings ask for a certificate of insurance before they allow contractors in. Keep a current copy ready to send and confirm with your insurer what the building needs. Do not describe coverage you do not hold. If you are unsure what a particular property manager requires, simply ask them before the first booking.

How long does it take to see results from a condo page?

It varies by city, competition and how well the page is built. Some owners notice better quality enquiries fairly quickly because the page answers questions in advance. Search visibility usually takes longer and depends on your profile, reviews and website. Track calls and forms from the page so you can judge it with real data.
