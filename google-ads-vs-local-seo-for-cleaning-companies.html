Google Ads vs Local SEO for Cleaning Companies

By Nakul Chandra, Founder and Lead Strategist · November 11, 2026 · 20 min read

Book a callEmail us

Almost every cleaning company owner we talk to asks the same thing sooner or later: should I pay for Google Ads, or should I put the effort into local SEO? Both put you in front of people who are searching for a cleaner. They work in very different ways, and choosing the wrong one for your situation is a fast way to waste a season and a good chunk of your patience.

This guide skips the theory and walks through real decisions. You will see four hypothetical owners in four different positions, a side by side comparison written as a list, a plan for your first 90 days on each route, the mistakes we see most often and a checklist you can use today.

Everything here is general guidance, not a promise of results. Your city, your services and your competition change the details, but the thinking holds up in almost every part of Canada, from a one van residential company in Saskatoon to a commercial crew in Toronto.

What Google Ads actually is

Google Ads lets you appear at the top of the search results when someone types a phrase like move out cleaning or office cleaning near me. You choose the searches, the areas, the days and the budget, and you pay when someone clicks your ad. You do not pay to be shown, only to be visited.

The big advantage is speed. A well built campaign can start sending people to your website within days, not months. You can also point it at one service, one neighbourhood or one slow week, which is hard to do with anything else.

The catch is that it is rented attention. The moment you pause the budget, the traffic stops, and nothing you paid for last month keeps working for you this month. It is a bit like hiring a sales rep on commission: useful, measurable and fast, but only as good as the account being managed and the page the clicks land on. Our Google Ads management service is built around that idea.

What local SEO actually is

Local SEO is the work of earning free visibility in two places: the map results that show a few businesses near the searcher, and the normal listings underneath. It covers your Google Business Profile, your service and city pages, your reviews and the consistent details that prove you are a real, local company.

It is slower. Most owners should expect weeks before small changes show up and months before the effort feels obvious. Nobody can promise a ranking, and anyone who does should make you nervous.

In return, the assets you build are yours. A good profile, a strong set of reviews and a clear page for each service keep working while you are on a job site, with no cost per click attached. Think of it as building a shopfront on a busy street, not renting a billboard. Our local SEO service starts with that shopfront before anything else.

Renting attention versus owning it

The cleanest way to think about the choice is rent or own. Ads rent you a spot at the top of the page for as long as you keep paying. Local SEO slowly builds a spot that you own, although nobody can guarantee exactly where it lands or how fast it arrives.

Renting is not a bad thing. If you need to fill a gap in your schedule next month, renting is exactly right. Owning is not automatically better either. If all you own is a half finished profile and three thin pages, you own very little, and a competitor with a stronger profile will keep beating you on the map.

Most healthy cleaning companies end up using both, with the balance shifting over time. The real question is not which one wins forever. It is which one deserves your first dollar and your first hour, given where you are standing right now. The rest of this post helps you answer that honestly.

How people really find a cleaner

Picture what actually happens. A homeowner in Ottawa needs a move out clean before keys change hands. She searches on her phone, sees a few ads at the top, a map with three businesses and then a list of websites. She may tap an ad, but she may just as easily scroll past it, because plenty of people prefer results that are not paid for.

Then she compares. She opens two or three profiles, reads recent reviews, glances at photos and checks whether the company actually serves her neighbourhood. The one that looks real, recent and responsive usually gets the call. Price matters, but trust and clarity often decide it first.

This matters for your choice because ads can get you into that comparison quickly, while local SEO and reviews help you win it. Both routes lead to the same moment: a stranger deciding whether to trust you with their home or office. Whatever you spend, spend it on being easy to trust.

Side by side comparison

Here is the plain comparison, one line at a time, so you can see where each route is strong and where it asks more of you.

  • Speed: Ads can start within days. Local SEO usually takes weeks to months to show anything you can feel.
  • Ongoing cost: Ads charge for every click and stop when you stop. SEO costs time and skill up front, then tends to keep producing without a per click charge.
  • Control: With ads you choose the exact search, area and hour. With SEO you influence the outcome but never fully control it.
  • Trust: Many people skip ads and prefer map and organic listings. Reviews and a strong profile carry more weight there.
  • Predictability: Ads are easier to switch on and off to match your schedule. SEO is steadier but slower to steer.
  • Skill needed: Ads punish careless setup quickly. SEO punishes impatience and shortcuts slowly.
  • Best for: Ads suit urgent gaps and specific services. SEO suits long term, repeat and trust driven work.

Neither list is a verdict. It is a map of trade-offs, and the next sections show how those trade-offs play out for different owners.

What each route costs you besides money

Owners often compare only the cash side, but each route also costs time and attention. Ads need someone to look at the account regularly, read the search terms and trim the waste. If nobody does, the budget drifts toward clicks that never turn into calls.

SEO costs a different kind of attention. Someone has to write real pages, keep the profile fresh, collect reviews and resist the urge to copy and paste. It is steady, unglamorous work, and it only pays off if it keeps going for months.

  • Ads ask for weekly attention and a willingness to cut what is not working.
  • SEO asks for monthly consistency and a tolerance for slow progress.
  • Both ask for a website that is clear on a phone.
  • Both ask for someone to answer calls and quotes quickly.
  • Both ask you to track results honestly, even when the numbers are disappointing.

Be realistic about which kind of attention you can actually give. A route you cannot maintain is the wrong route, however good it looks on paper.

Your website decides how both routes perform

Whichever route you pick, people end up on your website or your profile. If the site is slow, vague or awkward on a phone, ads burn money and SEO has little to work with. A visitor who cannot tell what you do, where you work or how to ask for a quote will simply go back and try the next company.

At a minimum, you want a clear page for each core service, a phone number that can be tapped, a short quote form and a few real photos and reviews. Those basics lift results from every source of traffic, paid or free. Our website design work focuses on exactly this, because traffic only pays off when the page turns a visit into a call.

If your site is not ready, start there before spending on either route. It is the least exciting advice in this post and probably the most valuable. A strong page makes every later dollar and every later hour work harder.

Example one: the brand new company

The following is a hypothetical example. Priya has just left a larger cleaning firm and started her own residential company in a mid-sized Ontario city. She has a van, a few referrals and a modest monthly budget. Her website is a single page, her Google Business Profile has not been verified yet and she has no reviews.

The tempting move is to jump straight into ads. But a new profile with no reviews and a one page site converts poorly, so her early clicks would be expensive and discouraging. Her better first step is the foundation: verify and complete the profile, build a proper page for her two main services and ask every early customer for a review. Our guide to getting more Google reviews fits this stage perfectly.

Once the page and profile are solid, Priya can add a small, tightly targeted ad campaign for one service in one area. That gives her quick leads while the slower work builds underneath. Her lesson: a new company usually needs the foundation first, then a small ad test, not the other way around.

Example two: the busy established company

The following is a hypothetical example. Marcus runs a residential and move out company with a small team in a prairie city. He is busy most of the year, but winter gets quiet and he leans heavily on word of mouth and a lead marketplace that takes a cut and shares each enquiry with competitors.

His real problem is dependence. Ads could fill his quiet weeks quickly, but he already has reviews, a decent profile and a reputation, which means SEO has real material to work with. His best plan is to improve his service and city pages, tidy his profile and run ads only around the seasonal dips and his most profitable service, such as move out cleaning.

That mix lets him reduce his reliance on the marketplace gradually. The seasonal marketing calendar helps him time the ads to the quiet stretches instead of spending evenly all year. His lesson: an established company often gets the best return from a blend, with ads aimed at specific gaps.

Example three: the commercial cleaner

The following is a hypothetical example. Dana runs a small commercial janitorial company in a large city. Her customers are property managers and office administrators who rarely search for a cleaner in a hurry. They compare a few companies, ask for references and take weeks to decide.

For her, broad ads for office cleaning tend to attract the wrong clicks, because many searchers are individuals or very small offices outside her target. Local SEO and a credible, detailed website matter more, along with a well written page that explains her process, her building access experience and her insurance. A narrow ad campaign can still help, but only for specific, high value searches.

Her first priority is a trustworthy commercial page and profile, which is why we point owners like her to the commercial janitorial marketing page before suggesting any spend. Her lesson: when the buyer takes weeks and wants proof, trust building beats volume.

Example four: the condo and move out specialist

The following is a hypothetical example. Sam runs a small team that focuses on condo turnovers and move out cleans in a busy downtown area. Work arrives in bursts around month end and the summer moving season, and the customers are often property managers, landlords and tenants in a hurry.

Searches in his category are urgent, so ads fit well. People typing move out cleaning today want an answer now and rarely wait for organic results to build. Sam can run a tight campaign for a few exact services and a small area, and send each click to a page about that exact job. Meanwhile, repeat relationships with property managers feed his reviews and profile.

He also has a long term play, because repeat landlords tend to remember who was reliable. Our guide to condo cleaning marketing covers this world in more depth. His lesson: urgent, specific work suits ads, while repeat relationships quietly build the organic side.

Your first 90 days on the Google Ads route

A sensible first quarter of ads is about learning cheaply, not scaling. The goal is to find out whether a small, focused campaign brings enquiries you can actually turn into jobs, without committing to a large budget before you know.

  1. Week one: Pick one service and one area to start. Write down what a good lead is worth to you so you know what a click should be worth.
  2. Week one: Set up call and form tracking before spending anything.
  3. Weeks two to four: Launch with a small budget, a handful of closely matched search phrases and a plain ad that names the service and area.
  4. Weeks four to eight: Review the search terms people actually used. Add the irrelevant ones as negatives and pause the ones that waste money.
  5. Weeks eight to twelve: Improve the landing page based on what you see, then decide whether to widen the area or add a second service.

At the end, you should know your approximate cost per enquiry and whether the work is worth continuing. That clarity is the real result of the first quarter.

Your first 90 days on the local SEO route

The SEO route starts with fixing what people see before you try to attract more of them. The early weeks are quiet on the surface, but they set up everything that follows, so resist the urge to skip ahead to the exciting parts.

  1. Weeks one to two: Claim and complete your Google Business Profile: category, services, service areas, hours and real photos.
  2. Weeks two to six: Build or improve one clear page for each core service, written in plain words and ending with an obvious next step.
  3. Weeks four to eight: Add a genuinely distinct page for each main city you serve, with real local detail instead of a swapped town name.
  4. Weeks six to ten: Start a steady review routine. Ask every happy customer, and reply to every review.
  5. Weeks ten to twelve: Look at which searches already bring people to your site and which pages they land on.

Do not expect a flood in 90 days. Expect a stronger base and early signs that it is working.

What to measure in the first 90 days

Clicks and impressions feel good, but they do not pay wages. The numbers that matter are the ones closest to a booked job. Decide in advance what you will count, write it down and look at it on the same day each month so you are not tempted to cherry pick.

  • Phone calls from the website and from your Google profile.
  • Quote form submissions, including how many were real jobs.
  • Which service or page each enquiry came from.
  • How many enquiries became booked work.
  • The question every new customer answers: how did you find us?
  • For ads, the cost of each enquiry once junk is removed.

Setting this up is not hard. Our guide to tracking calls and quote requests walks through it. Without tracking, you will end up arguing from gut feeling, and gut feeling tends to favour whichever channel you spent most recently.

Mistakes to avoid with Google Ads

Ads punish careless setup quickly, and the mistakes are surprisingly consistent. Most of them come from rushing to launch before deciding what the campaign should actually achieve.

  • Sending clicks to a home page that does not mention the service searched.
  • Targeting too wide an area, including places you do not serve.
  • Using broad phrases that attract job seekers, suppliers or do it yourself searchers.
  • Never reading the search terms report, so waste keeps building up.
  • Pausing the campaign after a few days because the first results look thin.
  • Ignoring missed calls. A paid click that rings out is the most expensive kind.

A narrow account with a clear page usually beats a wide one with a bigger budget. If you want help with the setup, start with a small, honest test, and ask whoever runs it to show you the search terms and the call results every month, not just a report full of clicks.

Mistakes to avoid with local SEO

SEO mistakes are quieter, because nothing blows up. You simply spend months doing work that does not help. The fixes are mostly about honesty, patience and detail.

  • Copying the same city page twenty times and changing only the town name.
  • Listing service areas you do not actually serve, which hurts trust and can cause trouble with Google.
  • Stuffing the same phrase into every sentence until the page reads badly.
  • Leaving reviews unanswered or asking for them only once a year.
  • Judging results after three weeks and changing everything.
  • Letting the profile go stale, with old hours and no new photos.

The biggest trap is thin city pages. If you serve several cities, our advice on city pages without copy and paste shows how to write each one so it earns its place. Quality and local detail beat volume every time, and they age far better.

Using both without doubling your workload

Once the basics are solid, the two routes start helping each other. Ads show you which phrases and pages produce real enquiries, which tells you where to invest your SEO effort. SEO builds the profile, reviews and pages that make your ad clicks more likely to convert.

The trick is not to treat them as separate projects. Use the same landing pages for both, the same tracking and the same review routine. Look at one simple report each month: how many calls and quote requests came in, and where from. One page of numbers is enough.

As organic enquiries grow, you can lower the ad spend on the phrases you already win and move that money to new services or slow weeks. That is the long term picture most owners are quietly aiming for: ads as a flexible tool, and organic visibility as the steady base underneath it.

How to size a first test without guessing

Owners often freeze because they cannot decide how much to try. You do not need a perfect number. You need a test that is small enough to survive being wrong and big enough to teach you something.

  1. Work out what one new regular customer is worth to you over a year, in rough terms.
  2. Decide what share of that you would happily spend to win them.
  3. Pick a monthly amount you could lose without stress, then run it for a full learning period.
  4. Choose one service and one area so the results are easy to read.
  5. Set a date to review, and decide in advance what result would make you continue, change or stop.

The same logic applies to SEO, except the currency is your hours, or the hours of whoever you hire. Decide how many hours a month you can protect, and commit to them. A modest, steady effort beats a burst of enthusiasm that fades by March.

A decision checklist you can use today

Answer these honestly and count the yeses on each side. There is no score to chase, only a direction to lean.

Lean toward Google Ads first if:

  • You need more work within the next few weeks.
  • You have one specific, profitable service to promote.
  • Your website already has a clear service page and a visible phone number.
  • You can measure calls and forms and will review them weekly.
  • Your budget is steady enough to let the campaign learn before you judge it.

Lean toward local SEO first if:

  • Your Google Business Profile is incomplete or unverified.
  • You have few reviews and no routine for collecting them.
  • Your customers value trust, such as recurring home or regular office cleaning.
  • You can wait months for compounding results.
  • You do not want your lead flow to depend on a monthly ad budget.

If you tick boxes on both sides, start with the foundation, then add a small ad test.

Doing it yourself or hiring help

Plenty of owners can handle the SEO basics themselves: completing a profile, asking for reviews and writing honest service pages. Those tasks reward knowing your business, and nobody knows it better than you. Ads are trickier to do casually, because the mistakes cost real money from the first week.

Hiring help makes sense when you do not have the hours, or when you would rather spend them on running jobs and your team. If you go that way, ask direct questions about reporting, contracts and who actually does the work. Our list of questions to ask before hiring an agency will help you keep the conversation honest.

Whatever you decide, keep ownership of your own accounts. Your Google Business Profile, your ad account and your website should be in your name, so you can walk away from any provider without losing what you built.

Keyword research for ads and SEO

Keyword research sounds technical, but it starts with a simple question: what would a customer type when they need exactly what you sell? Write down your services in plain words, such as deep cleaning, move out cleaning, post construction cleaning or office cleaning, and add the places you serve. Then add the words customers use that you might not, like maid service, janitorial or turnover clean.

Next, check those phrases in Google's free Keyword Planner and in the suggestions that appear as you type. Look for what people really search, which phrases are close variations and which ones signal someone ready to hire. A search like cleaning jobs near me is a worker, not a customer, and you do not want to pay for that click.

  • Group phrases by service, not by the order you thought of them.
  • Keep a separate list of phrases that clearly mean urgency or intent to hire.
  • Note the words that show the wrong audience, and save them for later.
  • Use the same research for your ad groups and your page titles.

One round of research feeds both routes, which is another reason they work well together.

Negative keywords, the quiet money saver

Negative keywords are searches you tell Google not to show your ad for. They are one of the most useful tools in an ad account, and one of the most neglected. Without them, a campaign for house cleaning can drift into searches for cleaning jobs, cleaning supplies, free cleaning checklists or cleaning courses.

Start with an obvious list: jobs, hiring, careers, training, course, diy, supplies, equipment and anything that shows someone wants to do the work or buy products, not hire you. Then, every week in the early months, read the search terms report and add new unwanted phrases as you spot them.

Also add negatives for services you do not offer. If you do not clean carpets, say so, and block carpet searches. If you do not serve a suburb, block it. This is slow, detailed work, but it is often the difference between a campaign that pays for itself and one that quietly drains a budget while looking busy.

Bids, daily budgets and how Google spends your money

Two settings confuse most owners: the bid and the daily budget. The bid is what you are willing to pay for a click, or the target you ask Google to aim for if you use automated bidding. The daily budget is the most Google will aim to spend on average in a day, and it can overshoot a little on busy days.

New accounts often work best with a small budget and a narrow focus, so the money is not spread across too many searches. Automated bidding can help once there is enough data, but it needs good conversion tracking, or it will happily optimise for clicks that mean nothing.

Do not guess what a click costs. Keyword Planner shows an estimated range for each phrase, and those ranges vary a lot by city and service, so check them for your own area before you commit. Treat them as rough guides to verify, then let a few weeks of real data tell you what is true.

What budgets and click costs look like, and how to verify them

Owners always ask how much they should spend, and any single number we gave you would be unreliable. Click costs for cleaning phrases differ between a large city and a small one, between residential and commercial work and between seasons. Competitors entering or leaving the market changes them again.

Instead of a number, use a method. Look up your main phrases in Keyword Planner and note the suggested range. Multiply by the number of clicks you would need to expect a handful of enquiries, using a cautious guess at how many visitors become calls. Compare that to the value of a new regular customer, as in the earlier sizing steps.

  • Check the ranges for your own city, not a national example.
  • Check them again after a month, because they move.
  • Expect a learning period before results settle.
  • Separate the budget for ads from the budget for your website and profile work.
  • Decide your stop and review date before you spend anything.

If the maths does not work at your volume, that is useful information, not a failure.

Local Services Ads and where they fit

Besides standard search ads, Google offers Local Services Ads in some categories and regions, shown at the very top with a badge and usually a screening or verification step. Availability depends on your service and location, so do not assume you can use them. Check Google's current eligibility pages for house cleaning, janitorial and the other services you offer in your own city.

Where they are available, the model differs from regular ads. You are typically paying for leads, such as calls or messages, not for every click, and your profile, reviews and responsiveness count for a lot. Missed calls and slow replies can hurt you, so you need to be ready to pick up.

Think of them as a bridge between paid and local. They reward the same things as local SEO: a complete profile, good reviews and a business that answers quickly. If they are open to you, they deserve a small test alongside, not instead of, your normal setup.

Google Business Profile and the map pack

The map pack is the short list of local businesses that appears above the normal results for many cleaning searches. For plenty of homeowners, it is the first and only place they look, which makes your Google Business Profile one of the most valuable assets you have. It is also free.

Google tends to weigh three things: how relevant your profile is to the search, how close you are to the searcher and how well known and trusted you appear. You cannot change where customers are standing, but you can control relevance and trust. Choose your primary category carefully, list every real service, write a clear description, add genuine photos and keep your hours accurate.

If you work from home and travel to customers, set it up as a service area business and list only the areas you actually serve. Our guide to service area profiles covers it step by step. This work supports ads, SEO and Local Services Ads at the same time.

Local listings, citations and reviews

Beyond Google, your business details appear on directories, social profiles and local listing sites. Consistency matters: the same business name, phone number, website and service area everywhere. When those details disagree, it confuses both customers and search engines.

Fix the obvious ones first: Google, Apple Maps, Bing, Facebook, your local Chamber of Commerce, and a handful of industry or neighbourhood directories that are genuinely used in your city. In Quebec, remember that many customers expect a French version of your details. You do not need hundreds of listings. A dozen accurate ones beat a hundred sloppy ones.

Reviews are the other half. Ask after each job while the result is fresh, make it easy with a direct link and reply to every review, good or bad. Never buy, invent or trade reviews. Our guide to getting more Google reviews includes ready to use wording. Reviews help SEO and they make every paid click more likely to convert.

Conversion tracking and the KPIs that matter

A conversion is any action that moves you toward a booked job: a call, a form, a click to message you or a quote request. Setting these up is what turns ads and SEO from guesswork into something you can judge. Without it, you can only see traffic, and traffic does not tell you whether the phone is ringing.

Set up Google Analytics 4 and tag your key actions as conversions, then connect it to your ad account and to Search Console. Our guides to Google Analytics 4 and tracking calls and quote requests cover the setup in plain language.

  • Enquiries per month, split by source.
  • Cost per enquiry on ads, after removing junk leads.
  • Share of enquiries that become booked jobs.
  • Calls and direction requests from your Google profile.
  • Searches and clicks in Search Console for your main pages.
  • Review count and average response time.

Pick five or six of these, not twenty. A short list you actually read beats a dashboard you ignore.

SEO timelines, month by month

SEO timelines vary, so treat this as a rough shape, not a schedule. In the first month or two, you are mostly fixing and building: the profile, the service pages, the tracking and the review routine. Visible change in enquiries is usually small, and that is normal.

Around months three to six, well made pages and a healthier profile often begin to earn more impressions and the first steady enquiries, especially for less competitive phrases and nearby areas. This is also when Search Console starts telling you something useful about what people are typing.

From about months six to twelve, consistent work can compound. More reviews, better pages and a stronger profile reinforce one another, and organic enquiries may become a meaningful part of your week. Competitive markets like big cities usually take longer than smaller ones. Nobody can promise an outcome, but owners who keep going tend to be glad they did, and those who stop at month two rarely see what they built.

Kevin runs a similar company in Calgary, which is also competitive but where moving seasons and the cold months shape demand differently.

Nadia knows that organic rankings in Toronto will take time and that neighbourhood level targeting matters, so she starts with a tight ad test in two neighbourhoods while building her profile and a page for each. Kevin leans on SEO first, because his profile is already strong, and uses ads for the busy spring and summer moving season.

After a quarter, Nadia has quick learning but higher pressure on her budget. Kevin has slower early numbers but steadier growth. Neither approach is better. Each fits the market and the owner, which is the whole point of deciding with your own numbers.

A 12 month combined plan

If you want one path that blends both routes, this is a reasonable shape for a year. Adjust it to your seasons, your city and your capacity, but keep the order, because later steps depend on earlier ones.

  1. Months one and two: Fix the website basics, complete your profile, set up tracking and begin asking for reviews.
  2. Month three: Launch a small ad test for one service and one area, and review the search terms weekly.
  3. Months four to six: Build service pages and distinct city pages. Trim wasted ad spend and keep the phrases that bring real enquiries.
  4. Months seven to nine: Add a second service to your ads if the first is paying off. Use Search Console to improve pages that are close to doing well.
  5. Months ten to twelve: Review a full year of enquiries by source. Shift budget toward what produced booked jobs and lower ad spend where organic has taken over.

The point is not perfection. It is a steady loop of building, measuring and adjusting.

A decision framework by budget

Without quoting numbers, you can still decide by how much room you have. Think in three bands: very tight, moderate and comfortable. Your own definition of each depends on your margins and your size, so use your gut about what hurts and what does not.

  • Very tight budget: Put almost everything into the free foundation: profile, reviews, service pages and tracking. Hold off on ads until you can run a proper test without strain.
  • Moderate budget: Do the foundation first, then run a narrow ad test on one profitable service. Keep it small and review it on a fixed date.
  • Comfortable budget: Run both together from the start, with ads aimed at specific gaps and a steady monthly SEO effort. Consider Local Services Ads if you are eligible.

In every band, the website and profile come first, and tracking is not optional. If you are unsure which band you are in, begin at the cautious end. Our pricing page shows what the website work involves, and you can scale up from there once the numbers support it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I run Google Ads and local SEO at the same time?

Yes, and many cleaning companies do. The key is to share the same landing pages and tracking so the two efforts support each other. Start small on both rather than splitting a thin budget across everything. If money is tight, build the profile and service pages first, then add a narrow ad test for one service.

How long should I give Google Ads before judging it?

Give a new campaign several weeks of steady running before drawing conclusions, because it needs time to gather data and you need time to remove wasted searches. Judge it on calls, quote requests and booked jobs, not clicks. If after a fair test the enquiries do not justify the spend, change the targeting or the page before quitting.

Is local SEO worth it for a very small cleaning company?

Often yes, because the basics cost little money and mostly cost effort. A complete Google Business Profile, a clear service page and a habit of asking for reviews can help a one van business look credible. You will not get overnight results, but the work stays with you rather than disappearing when a budget ends.

Why would my ads waste money even with a good budget?

The most common reasons are loose targeting, such as showing for searches you do not serve, and landing pages that do not match what the person searched for. Missed calls and no tracking also hide the real picture. A narrow account with a clear page usually does better than a wide one with a larger budget.

Do reviews help Google Ads too?

Indirectly, yes. Reviews do not directly set your ad position, but they make people more willing to call after they click. A visitor who sees a solid review profile and a clear page is more likely to enquire. That means your ad money goes further without changing a single keyword.

Should a commercial cleaner use the same approach as a residential one?

Not usually. Commercial buyers take longer, compare several companies and care about references, insurance and process. Broad ads often pull in the wrong clicks. A credible commercial page, a strong profile and targeted searches tend to matter more than high volume traffic. Test narrow ads only for specific, valuable searches.

What should I do first if my website is weak?

Fix the website first, because both routes send people to it. Make sure each main service has its own page, the phone number is easy to find on a phone and there is a short quote form. Without that, ads waste money and SEO has very little to build on.

How do I know whether Google Ads or SEO brought a customer?

Set up call tracking and form tracking, and ask every new customer how they found you. Compare the answers with your reports. Over a few months, you will see which channel produces the jobs you want. Do not rely on clicks alone, because many people look first and call later.
