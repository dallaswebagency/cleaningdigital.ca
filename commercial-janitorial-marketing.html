Problems we solve

Where commercial and janitorial companies lose enquiries.

The website looks like a residential cleaner

A homepage built around a smiling cleaner with a spray bottle and a booking button does not speak to a facility manager. Commercial buyers want to see scope, schedules, insurance and the types of buildings you handle. A residential look signals that you may not fully understand the job.

There is no clear way to ask for a walkthrough

Commercial work is quoted after someone looks at the space. If the only option is a generic contact form, the request feels vague and a bit risky. A page that explains how a site visit works, and what happens afterwards, makes starting a conversation low stakes.

Proof is missing or hidden

Buyers look for insurance details, safety training, references and the kinds of sites you already clean. Without them, you are asking a stranger to take your word for it. Even a short list of building types and an honest description of your process helps a great deal.

Every sector is lumped together

A clinic, a school, a warehouse and a condo corporation have very different needs. One generic services list does not speak to any of them. Sector pages give each buyer a reason to believe that you understand their particular building and their particular worries.

Leads depend on bidding platforms

Competing on tender sites and marketplace leads pushes the conversation straight to price. Owning your search presence and your references means some buyers find you first, before any comparison begins, and the first conversation starts from trust instead.

How commercial and janitorial companies win work online

Commercial cleaning is bought differently from almost everything else in this trade. The person searching is often a property manager, office administrator, facility lead or owner with a short list and a bad experience behind them. They are not after a one-off clean. They want a contractor who shows up every night, keeps staff consistent and does not create problems that land on their desk. Your website is read as evidence of how organized you will be once the contract starts. That is a high bar, and most buyers set it before they ever call.

Searches tend to be specific and a little cautious. Janitorial services for a small office, cleaning for a medical building, contract cleaner for a warehouse, replacing a current provider. Many buyers do not mention price at all at first. They look for insurance, references, a clear scope of work and a way to arrange a walkthrough. If those are missing, they assume the same gaps will show up in the service itself. A page that covers all of that in plain language earns a call back instead of a quick close of the tab.

The decision usually involves more than one person. An office manager finds you, a director signs off, and sometimes a head office or building owner has to agree as well. So your page needs to work for a skimmer and for a careful reader who forwards it to a boss. A short summary up top, detail underneath and a contact route that leads to a person who can book a site visit all help. Write the page so that the forwarded version still makes sense when the boss has not seen anything else on the site.

Buying cycles are slower and the contracts are longer, which changes what marketing is for. A single good contract can be worth far more than a dozen small jobs, so a handful of well-qualified enquiries a month can be plenty. The goal is fewer, better conversations, started by a site that makes a stranger feel safe handing over the keys to the building. Every part of the site should support that feeling of safety and order. Trust is the whole sale here, so build it early.

Across Canada

Commercial cleaning looks different depending on where you are. In large cities, downtown towers, medical clinics and co-working spaces dominate, and buyers often have a property management firm making the decisions. In smaller cities, a handful of local businesses, schools and municipal buildings may make up much of the market, and word of mouth carries more weight. Your website should say which of these worlds you actually work in, so the right buyer recognizes you. The same service can be sold very differently in each setting, and the page should show that you know which one is yours.

Climate shapes the work as well. Winter in much of Canada means salt, slush and grit tracked into entrances every day, so matting, floor care and entrance cleaning matter more than in milder regions. On the coast, damp conditions bring their own concerns about floors and air quality. Buyers notice when a contractor already understands what their lobby looks like in February and has a plan for it. That sort of local, specific detail is what separates a contractor who has done the work from one who only claims to.

Building age and rules also vary. Older downtown buildings, new industrial parks, shared strata properties and heritage sites all bring different access, security and scheduling needs. If you serve more than one region, build pages that reflect that rather than one national template. The Canada overview shows how we think about planning across several cities and provinces. Do not guess at what a region wants. Ask your own crews what their sites look like on a normal Tuesday and write from that.

How do commercial cleaning buyers find a contractor?

Many start with a search for a specific need, such as janitorial services for a clinic, then check the map listings and a few websites. Others ask colleagues or building managers for a name. Both routes lead back to your site, so it has to look credible within seconds.

What should a janitorial website show?

The types of buildings you clean, your scope and schedules, insurance and safety information, how a site visit works and a way to request one. References or a short description of existing relationships help. Avoid cartoonish stock photos that make the company look small or unprepared.

Do I need a page for each industry I serve?

Where you have real experience, yes. A dental clinic manager and a warehouse supervisor care about different things. Each page should describe scope, schedules and special requirements honestly. If you have never cleaned a particular sector, leave it off rather than pretend to know it.

Are Google Ads useful for commercial cleaning?

They can be, because a single contract can be valuable and searches often show strong intent. Volume is low, so tight targeting matters. We set up campaigns for specific services and areas and review the search terms regularly to cut waste and keep the spend honest.

Can you help us stop competing on price?

Marketing cannot remove price comparison, but it can change who reaches you first. A clear scope, proof of reliability and good references mean some buyers start the conversation on trust instead of on the cheapest quote. That alone changes the kind of work you win.

How should we handle references and testimonials?

Only use real ones, and always with permission. Many commercial clients prefer to give a reference by phone rather than appear on a website. A short description of the kinds of sites you serve and an offer to share references on request is a good middle path.

What does a good walkthrough request form ask?

Name, company, building type, approximate size, current cleaning schedule and the best time to talk. Keep it short. Too many fields scare people off, and you can collect everything else during the visit itself, when you are standing in the building and can see it.

How long before a commercial site starts bringing enquiries?

It depends on your starting point and your competition. Profile and page fixes can help within weeks, while search rankings build over months. Commercial enquiries are fewer by nature, so we measure quality and walkthrough requests each month, not raw clicks or traffic. We would rather show you real enquiries than a chart of impressions.
